Executive summary

The GCC healthcare market — public and private spending on hospitals, clinics, diagnostics and related services across Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Oman and Bahrain — is one of the fastest-growing healthcare markets in the world relative to its population. Published estimates for 2025 range from USD 118 bn to USD 129 bn depending on whether pharmaceuticals, devices and insurance are counted alongside services; our central estimate is USD 121 bn, growing at about 7% a year to USD 194 bn by 2032.

Three forces drive the growth: population growth and ageing in a region where chronic disease prevalence (diabetes, cardiovascular, obesity) is among the highest globally; government programmes that shift delivery to the private sector while keeping the state as the dominant payer (Saudi Vision 2030 and the Health Sector Transformation Program; UAE mandatory insurance in Abu Dhabi, Dubai and now the northern emirates); and capacity expansion — thousands of new beds planned or under construction across the region, much of it private or PPP.

Saudi Arabia is the largest market by far, followed by the UAE; the two together account for roughly three-quarters of regional spend. For suppliers, the GCC is a high-price, high-specification, tender-driven market where registration, local distribution and insurer relationships decide access.

Scope and definitions

Included: hospital and clinic services (inpatient, outpatient, day-care), diagnostics and laboratory services, long-term and home care, and the associated government and private health expenditure. Reported separately in our other reports: pharmaceuticals, medical devices, health insurance premiums and health IT. Some publishers fold these into "healthcare market" totals, which explains the wider range of published figures.

Key facts and context

  • GCC CHE was 5.0% of GDP in 2024, projected to reach 5.7% by 2029 (Alpen Capital)
  • GCC hospital bed density 20.8 beds per 10,000 population (end-2024), above the global average per GCC-Stat (Times of Oman / GCC-Stat)
  • Government-owned beds account for 73% of GCC bed capacity (Times of Oman / GCC-Stat)
  • Nursing staff make up 39.3% of the 1.05 million GCC healthcare workforce (Times of Oman / GCC-Stat)
  • GCC saw 400+ healthcare M&A/investment transactions 2021-2025 (UAE 198, Saudi 170) (PharmaKnowl)
  • GCC added 1,846 hospital beds 2020-2022 to reach 133,731 beds (Alpen Capital older edition) (AGBI)
  • Frost & Sullivan expects ~30% of hospital investment to go to digital health infrastructure over the next decade (AGBI)
  • Burjeel Holdings signed an MoU with Saudi Ministry of Investment to invest up to USD 1 bn in Saudi hospitals/clinics (Aug 2022); Burjeel then had 1,660 beds across UAE and Oman (Gulf News)
  • PureHealth's Daman insured 3.4 million members with H1 2026 GWP of AED 5.5 bn (Khaleej Times)

GCC and India outlook

Saudi Arabia

The Health Sector Transformation Program is corporatising public hospitals into regional health clusters, expanding private provision and PPP, and privatising non-core services. NUPCO centralises purchasing for the public sector; private groups (Dr Sulaiman Al Habib, Mouwasat, Dallah, Fakeeh, Al Hammadi) are listed and expanding. Giga-projects (NEOM, Red Sea, Qiddiya) carry health components.

UAE

Mandatory insurance (Abu Dhabi 2006, Dubai 2014, federal expansion from 2025) made private providers the majority of outpatient volume. Consolidation into large groups (PureHealth/SEHA, Burjeel, NMC, Mediclinic, Aster, Emirates Health Services) and specialist capacity (oncology, cardiac, paediatric) are the growth themes; Abu Dhabi and Dubai compete as medical-tourism hubs.

Qatar, Kuwait, Oman, Bahrain

Qatar is public-led (Hamad Medical Corporation) with growing private capacity; Kuwait is mid-way through a large public hospital build-out; Oman is opening the sector to private investment under Vision 2040; Bahrain has introduced a national health insurance scheme (Sehati).

Relevance for India

GCC hospital groups source clinicians, nurses, pharmaceuticals, consumables and increasingly devices from India; Indian hospital groups are entering the region (Aster, Apollo, Medanta partnerships). The two markets are linked by workforce, supply chain and patient flows.

Market dynamics

Growth drivers

  • Growing and ageing population: share aged 50+ rising from 12.7% (2024) to 13.8% (2029) (Alpen Capital)
  • Expansion of mandatory health insurance and rising insurance penetration across GCC states (Alpen Capital)
  • High NCD burden: NCDs >70% of deaths; diabetes prevalence 11.8% (Oman) to >20% (Kuwait, Bahrain) (PharmaKnowl/WHO)
  • Government privatisation and PPP programmes (e.g., Saudi plan to privatise 290 hospitals; 65% private delivery target)
  • Medical inflation and rising treatment costs lifting CHE to 5.7% of GDP by 2029

Restraints

  • Heavy dependence on expatriate workforce and imported supplies (85-90% device import dependence)
  • Oman's slow CHE growth (4.0% CAGR) reflecting fiscal constraints in smaller states
  • Public sector still holds 73% of beds, limiting private capacity growth in some markets
  • Rising cost pressure on insurers and employers as premiums climb

Opportunities

  • 12,317 new beds required 2024-2029, mostly expected from the private sector
  • Medical tourism growth and specialised-care demand (oncology, cardiology, post-acute)
  • Digital health: GCC digital health revenue USD 1.83 bn in 2025; Kuwait HIE linking 28 public hospitals; UAE unified licensing platform (2026)
  • Cross-border consolidation: PureHealth (Circle Health UK, HHG Greece/Cyprus), Burjeel Saudi expansion, Amanat post-acute platform

Challenges

  • Bed density of 20.8 per 10,000 remains below high-income benchmarks despite growth
  • Workforce shortages: need for 6.8% annual workforce growth; reliance on recruitment from abroad
  • Localisation requirements (Saudi 40% local content minimum for government tenders) raising supplier compliance cost
  • Competition intensifying as large groups acquire smaller operators (AGBI)

What it means for you

  • Operators and investors: private capacity is still growing, but the easy outpatient growth is done in the UAE; the opportunities are specialist tertiary care, day surgery, home care, long-term care and Saudi secondary cities. Design central procurement and insurer contracting into the operating model from day one.
  • Suppliers: registration (SFDA, MOHAP/DoH/DHA), a local partner and insurer-formulary presence are prerequisites; central tenders (NUPCO, SEHA/PureHealth, DHA) reward structured quotations and reliable supply over the lowest price.
  • Hospital leaders: insurer price pressure and medical inflation make procurement and supply chain the margin lever; see our procurement advisory.
